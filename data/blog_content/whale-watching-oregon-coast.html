You can see gray whales from Oregon's headlands in every month of the year, but the big show comes twice: the southbound migration from mid-December through mid-January, and the northbound return from late March through June. Watching from shore is free, and the best whale watching Oregon coast spots are high, fenced viewpoints like the Depoe Bay seawall, Cape Perpetua and Yaquina Head. This guide covers when to go, where to stand, how to spot a blow, which boat tours run out of Depoe Bay and Newport, and what to expect at Whale Watch Week.

Quick answer: whale watching on the Oregon coast at a glance

  • Winter migration: mid-December to mid-January, usually peaking between Christmas and New Year's Day, when roughly 30 whales an hour can pass at the peak.
  • Spring migration: late March through June. Northbound whales, including mothers with calves, often swim within half a mile of shore.
  • Summer residents: about 200 gray whales feed off Oregon from June to mid-November, especially around Depoe Bay and Newport.
  • Whale Watch Week: Oregon State Parks volunteers help visitors spot whales at 14-15 coastal sites, 10 a.m. to 1 p.m., the week after Christmas and the last week of March. Free.
  • Best tours: one- to two-hour boat trips from Depoe Bay harbor start around $30 per adult.

When Is Whale Watching Season on the Oregon Coast?

Whale watching season on the Oregon coast runs all year, with three distinct windows: the winter migration (mid-December to mid-January), the spring migration (late March through June) and the summer feeding season (June to mid-November). Each one offers a different kind of viewing, so the best time depends on what you want to see.

Winter: the southbound migration (mid-December to mid-January)

Gray whales spend summer feeding in the Bering and Chukchi seas off Alaska, then swim south to the calving lagoons of Baja California, Mexico. The round trip can cover roughly 10,000 to 14,000 miles. According to the Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife, the height of the winter migration off Oregon is usually between Christmas and New Year's Day, and numbers stay strong through January. At the peak, around 30 whales an hour can pass a good viewpoint.

The catch: southbound whales are in a hurry and travel about 5 miles offshore, so you'll mostly see distant blows. Bring binoculars and pick a high viewpoint. Our Oregon coast storm watching guide covers how to read the surf forecast.

Spring: the northbound return (late March through June)

The return trip is slower and closer to land. Adults pass first, starting in late February and March, and mothers with calves follow from April into June after staying on the breeding grounds an extra month or two. It's not unusual to see them within half a mile of shore, which makes spring the best season for clear looks at backs, flukes and the occasional breach. Spring Whale Watch Week lines up with Oregon's spring break.

Summer and fall: the resident whales (June to mid-November)

About 200 gray whales skip the full trip to Alaska and spend the summer feeding along the Oregon coast. Oregon State Parks calls the central coast a hot spot for whales from June to mid-November. They often feed in rocky coves right below viewpoints in Depoe Bay, Newport and Lincoln City, so summer offers the closest views of the year, along with calmer seas for boat tours.

Whale Watch Week 2026-27: Dates and How It Works

Oregon State Parks holds Whale Watch Week twice a year: in winter, the week between Christmas and New Year's, and in spring, during the last week of March. Trained volunteers and park rangers stand at viewpoints along the coast from 10 a.m. to 1 p.m. daily, help visitors spot whales and answer questions. It's free.

  • Winter 2025: Saturday, Dec. 27 through Wednesday, Dec. 31, at 14 sites, per the Oregon State Parks announcement.
  • Spring 2026: Saturday, March 21 through Sunday, March 29, at 15 sites.
  • Winter 2026: as of mid-November, Oregon State Parks had not yet announced this year's dates. The event has run Dec. 27-31 in recent years; check the Oregon State Parks whale watching page for the official dates and site map.

Oregon State Parks recruits and trains up to 400 volunteers for the two weeks each year, and the Whale Watching Center in Depoe Bay stays open longer hours (10 a.m. to 4 p.m.) during both events. During winter Whale Watch Week, the center has reported as many as 50 whales a day.

Tip: Whale Watch Week falls during the coast's busiest holiday stretch. If you plan to stay overnight, book lodging early, and see our guide to Christmas in Oregon for other holiday events along the way.

Best Whale Watching Spots on the Oregon Coast

The best whale watching spots on the Oregon coast are high headlands that jut into the ocean: Depoe Bay, Cape Perpetua, Yaquina Head, Cape Meares, Ecola State Park and Cape Lookout. Height lets you see blows farther out, and headlands put you closer to the whales' path. Here are the standouts from north to south.

Ecola State Park (Cannon Beach)

The closest top spot to Portland, about a 90-minute drive. Ecola and Indian Beach viewpoints just north of Cannon Beach look out over sea stacks and Tillamook Rock Lighthouse. A day-use parking permit is required.

Neahkahnie Mountain viewpoint (Manzanita)

A highway turnout on US 101 between mileposts 41 and 42, about 13 miles south of Cannon Beach and just north of Manzanita. It gives a high, wide view straight from the car.

Cape Meares and Cape Lookout (Tillamook County)

West of Tillamook, the Cape Meares Lighthouse viewpoint sits about 200 feet above the ocean. To the south, Cape Lookout juts about 2 miles out to sea; the 2.5-mile trail to its tip is one of the best spring whale walks in the state, though it is muddy in winter.

Boiler Bay, Depoe Bay and Rocky Creek (Lincoln County)

This short stretch of US 101 is Oregon's whale watching capital. Boiler Bay State Scenic Viewpoint, the Depoe Bay seawall and Rocky Creek State Scenic Viewpoint all overlook rocky reefs where resident whales feed. Cape Foulweather, a few miles south, rises about 500 feet above the sea.

Yaquina Head Outstanding Natural Area (Newport)

North of Newport, Yaquina Head is home to Oregon's tallest lighthouse and is managed by the Bureau of Land Management. Resident gray whales often feed just off the rocks in summer, and the headland makes a fine winter viewpoint. There's a per-vehicle entrance fee; federal recreation passes are accepted.

Cape Perpetua (Yachats)

Just south of Yachats, the Cape Perpetua Scenic Area, run by the Siuslaw National Forest, has the highest drive-up viewpoint on the Oregon coast at about 800 feet. The visitor center and Cook's Chasm turnout below are also good.

Florence and the south coast

The US 101 turnout near Sea Lion Caves north of Florence looks out on Heceta Head Lighthouse. Farther south, try Shore Acres State Park near Coos Bay, Face Rock viewpoint in Bandon, Cape Blanco near Port Orford (the westernmost point in Oregon), Cape Sebastian, Cape Ferrelo and Harris Beach State Park in Brookings.

Whale Watching in Depoe Bay and the Whale Watching Center

Depoe Bay is the best single town for whale watching in Oregon because resident gray whales feed just off its seawall from late spring to fall, and boat tours reach open ocean within minutes of leaving its tiny harbor. In winter, the town's rocky shoreline also puts you close to the migration route.

The Oregon State Parks Whale Watching Center sits on the seawall along US 101 in downtown Depoe Bay. The 1956 building, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2012, has big ocean-facing windows, whale exhibits, loaner binoculars and rangers or volunteers who can point out blows. Admission is free.

2026 roof repairs: the center and the viewing area around it closed in August 2026 so crews could replace the original roofing membrane and drains. Oregon State Parks planned to finish by the end of October, ahead of the winter migration. Before you go, check the construction advisories on the Whale Watching Center park page or call 541-765-3304. If it's closed, the turnout just north of the center, Boiler Bay, Rocky Creek and Cape Foulweather are all nearby.

Oregon Coast Whale Watching Tours

Most Oregon coast whale watching tours leave from Depoe Bay, with a larger cruise boat operating from Newport. Trips last one to two hours and are canceled or rescheduled when the bar is too rough. Prices below are from each company's website in November 2026 and can change.

Whale's Tail Charters (Depoe Bay)

Small rigid-hull inflatable (Zodiac-style) boats that carry 2 to 6 passengers, for a fast, low-to-the-water ride. The company lists a 1-hour whale watch at $48 and a 1.5-hour trip at $58, and says it gives a full refund or reschedule if the ocean is too rough.

How to Spot a Whale From Shore

To spot a whale, look for the blow: a column of misty spray up to 15 feet high that hangs in the air for about five seconds on a calm day. Gray whales usually surface three to five times in a row before a longer dive, so once you see one blow, keep watching the same patch of water.

  • Go early on a calm day. ODFW recommends a calm morning. Wind whips whitecaps that look a lot like blows. Morning light with the sun at your back is best.
  • Get up high. The higher the viewpoint, the farther you can see. Headlands beat beaches.
  • Scan with your eyes first. Sweep the horizon slowly without binoculars, then lift them to the spot where you saw spray.
  • Know what you're looking at. Adult gray whales grow up to about 50 feet long and 80,000 pounds; calves are about 15 feet long at birth. They're mottled gray with no dorsal fin, just a row of "knuckles" along the back.
  • Watch for other whales. Gray whales are the stars, but humpback whales, orcas and even blue whales have been seen off Depoe Bay.
  • Dress for the coast. Even on a clear winter day, wind on a headland feels cold. Pack layers, a rain shell, binoculars and a thermos. See our guide to Oregon weather by season.

How Many Gray Whales Are There Now?

NOAA Fisheries' latest count, released in July 2026, estimates about 17,750 eastern North Pacific gray whales (a range of 15,930 to 20,530), up from about 12,890 the year before. That earlier figure was one of the lowest since counts began in the late 1960s.

The population peaked at roughly 27,000 whales in 2016, then fell sharply. NOAA declared an Unusual Mortality Event from 2019 to 2023 after many whales stranded thin and malnourished, and scientists point to changes in the Bering Sea, where shrinking sea ice has reduced the seafloor prey the whales depend on. The NOAA Fisheries report details the new count, and scientists speaking at a September 2026 gray whale symposium in Newport cautioned that the whales are not yet out of the woods.

Safety and Etiquette

Watch whales from paved viewpoints and fenced overlooks, not from wet rocks, jetties or logs on the beach. Winter whale season overlaps with storm season, and sneaker waves can surge far up the beach without warning. Never turn your back on the ocean.

  • Check the National Weather Service forecast for high surf warnings and wind before you go. On the south coast, use NWS Medford.
  • Check TripCheck for road conditions on US 101 and the Coast Range passes (US 26, OR 18, US 20).
  • Pull completely off US 101 at turnouts, and watch for pedestrians along the Depoe Bay seawall.
  • Federal law protects marine mammals. Boats keep their distance, and you should never approach or feed a whale, seal or sea lion. Report stranded marine mammals to the Oregon State Police tip line at 800-452-7888 (see ODFW's stranding page).

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best month for whale watching on the Oregon coast?

Late December is best for numbers: the winter migration usually peaks between Christmas and New Year's Day, when about 30 whales an hour can pass. April and May are best for close views of northbound whales and calves, and July through September is best for resident whales feeding near shore and calm seas for boat tours.

Where is the best place to see whales on the Oregon coast?

Depoe Bay is the best overall, thanks to its seawall, the Whale Watching Center and resident whales that feed close to shore. Cape Perpetua near Yachats offers the highest drive-up view, Yaquina Head near Newport is great in summer, and Ecola State Park near Cannon Beach is the closest top spot to Portland.

When is Whale Watch Week in Oregon?

Oregon State Parks holds Winter Whale Watch Week the week between Christmas and New Year's (Dec. 27-31 in recent years) and Spring Whale Watch Week the last week of March (March 21-29 in 2026). Volunteers are at viewpoints from 10 a.m. to 1 p.m. Check the Oregon State Parks website for this year's dates.

Can you see whales from shore in Oregon?

Yes. Most whale watching in Oregon happens from shore, and it's free. Choose a high headland, go on a calm morning and bring binoculars. In spring and summer, whales often swim within half a mile of shore.

How much does a whale watching tour cost in Depoe Bay?

In 2026, Dockside Charters lists 1-hour trips at $30 for adults and $15 for children 12 and under, and 2-hour trips at $50 and $25. Whale's Tail Charters lists small-boat trips at $48 for 1 hour and $58 for 1.5 hours. Prices change, so confirm with the operator.

Are there orcas off the Oregon coast?

Sometimes. Orcas are seen off Oregon occasionally, often in spring when transient killer whales follow migrating gray whale calves, but sightings are unpredictable. Humpback whales are also seen, mostly in summer and fall. Gray whales are by far the most common.
