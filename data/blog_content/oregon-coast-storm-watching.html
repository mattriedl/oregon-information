When the jet stream dips south and Pacific storms line up offshore, the coast turns into the most dramatic show in the state. Oregon coast storm watching means standing safely on a cliff-top viewpoint (or sitting beside an oceanfront fireplace) while winter swells explode into plumes of spray that can top 100 feet. This guide covers the best places for storm watching on the Oregon coast, when the season peaks, how to read a storm forecast, how to stay safe and where to book a room with a front-row view.

Quick answer: Oregon coast storm watching at a glance

  • Season: November through March, peaking in December and January.
  • Best single spot: Shore Acres State Park near Coos Bay, which has a fully enclosed observation building.
  • Best town for storm watching: Depoe Bay for the spouting horns, or Yachats for oceanfront lodging next to Cape Perpetua.
  • Best days: swells of 15+ feet, especially when they coincide with a high tide or the winter King Tides.
  • Golden rule: watch from elevated, fenced viewpoints. Never go onto beaches, jetties or wet rocks during high surf.

When Is Storm Watching Season in Oregon?

Storm watching season on the Oregon coast runs from November through March, with the biggest and most frequent storms in December and January. The first strong swells often arrive in late October, and a few powerful systems can still roll through in early April. Weekdays in January and February are the quietest time to visit, and lodging rates are usually at their lowest of the year.

These storms are born as low-pressure systems over the North Pacific. Strong winds blowing across thousands of miles of open ocean build long-period swells that travel to Oregon and slam into its basalt headlands and sandstone cliffs. Winter is also the rainy season: the coast gets most of its 70 to 100+ inches of annual rain between November and March (see our guide to Oregon weather by season), so expect wind, rain and the occasional sunbreak, often within the same hour.

How to Pick a Good Storm-Watching Day

The biggest wave shows don't always happen in the middle of the storm. Often the best viewing is the day after a front passes, when the rain eases but the swell is still huge. Before you go, check:

  • Swell height and period: The National Weather Service Portland (north and central coast) and NWS Medford (south coast) issue surf forecasts. Swells of 15 feet or more with a period of 14+ seconds carry enough energy for spectacular displays. A High Surf Warning means great viewing from safe spots and real danger on the beach.
  • Tide tables: Waves reach farther up the cliffs and into spouting horns around high tide. NOAA's Tides & Currents site has predictions for every coastal station.
  • Road conditions: Winter storms can bring downed trees, flooding and slides on Highway 101 and the routes over the Coast Range. Check ODOT TripCheck before you drive.

King Tides: The Peak of the Action

King Tides are the highest tides of the year. They happen when a new or full moon lines up with the moon's closest approach to Earth, and in Oregon they arrive for a few days each November, December and January. When a winter storm coincides with a King Tide, waves surge onto areas that are normally dry, which makes for the most dramatic wave displays of the season and a preview of future sea-level rise. The Oregon King Tides Project publishes each winter's dates and invites visitors to share photos as community science.

Best Places for Storm Watching on the Oregon Coast

Nearly all of the Oregon Coast's 363 miles of shoreline are public. These viewpoints combine big wave action with elevated, hardened places to stand, from north to south.

Ecola State Park and Cannon Beach (North Coast)

In Cannon Beach, Haystack Rock stands alone against the surf in winter. For a safer, higher view, drive into Ecola State Park, whose bluffs look out to Tillamook Rock Light ("Terrible Tilly"), a decommissioned lighthouse about a mile offshore that waves sometimes wash over during big storms.

Seaside Promenade

The 1.5-mile Promenade in Seaside is a paved, elevated walkway set back from the surf line, which makes it an easy and accessible spot to watch storm waves roll across the wide beach. It's also a short walk from restaurants and hotels.

Boiler Bay and Rocky Creek (Central Coast)

Between Lincoln City and Depoe Bay, the Boiler Bay State Scenic Viewpoint sits on a basalt shelf where waves crash into a rocky cove. The rusted boiler of the J. Marhoffer, which burned and wrecked here in 1910, is visible at low tide. A few miles south, Rocky Creek State Scenic Viewpoint offers a forested bluff with offshore rocks that light up in big swells.

Depoe Bay: Spouting Horns Right Downtown

Depoe Bay may be the easiest place on the coast for storm watching. The seawall runs right along Highway 101, so you can watch from the sidewalk. When waves surge into lava-rock fissures, the town's spouting horns shoot plumes of seawater roughly 20 to 40 feet into the air, sometimes soaking the sidewalk and passing cars. The Oregon State Parks Whale Watching Center on the seawall offers an indoor viewing room with big windows.

Devils Punchbowl and Yaquina Head

At Otter Rock, north of Newport, Devils Punchbowl State Natural Area overlooks a collapsed sea cave where incoming waves churn and boil. Just north of Newport, Yaquina Head Outstanding Natural Area offers views of storm surf around Oregon's tallest lighthouse.

Cape Perpetua and Yachats

Just south of Yachats, the Cape Perpetua Scenic Area is home to Thor's Well and the Spouting Horn at Cook's Chasm. Thor's Well is a bowl-shaped hole in the basalt shelf that appears to swallow the sea and then erupts as waves surge up from below. It is also one of the most dangerous places on the coast during high surf, so watch from the paved paths and the overlook above, never from the rock shelf.

Heceta Head Lighthouse (near Florence)

North of Florence, Heceta Head Lighthouse sits on a headland that takes the full force of winter swells. The viewpoint along Highway 101 offers a classic view of the lighthouse beam cutting through storm clouds.

Shore Acres State Park Storm Watching Guide

Shore Acres State Park, about 13 miles southwest of Coos Bay on the Cape Arago Highway, is widely considered the best place for storm watching in Oregon. Its tilted sandstone cliffs force incoming swells upward, and during big winter storms the spray regularly shoots more than 100 feet into the air, higher than the bluffs themselves.

  • Where to watch: The fully enclosed observation building stands on the site of timber baron Louis J. Simpson's former mansion, giving you a dry, windproof view. Walled clifftop viewpoints and paths extend along the bluff.
  • When to go: Aim for a day with a large swell (15+ feet) around high tide. Mornings after a storm front passes often combine huge waves with better light.
  • Parking: A day-use parking permit is required. Check Oregon State Parks for current fees and hours.
  • Combine it with: Neighboring Sunset Bay and Cape Arago state parks along the same road. From Thanksgiving through New Year's Eve, the park's formal gardens host the popular Holiday Lights display, so you can storm watch by day and see the lights after dark.
  • Stay nearby: Coos Bay, North Bend and Bandon have the closest lodging. See Coos County for more nearby towns.

Is Storm Watching in Oregon Safe?

Storm watching in Oregon is safe when you watch from elevated, fenced or enclosed viewpoints, and dangerous when you go onto beaches, jetties or rocks. Sneaker waves, rolling logs and sudden surges injure and kill visitors along the coast almost every winter. Follow these rules from Oregon State Parks and coastal safety officials:

  • Never turn your back on the ocean. Sneaker waves are much larger than the waves before them and can surge far up the beach without warning, even on calmer days.
  • Stay away from logs. Waves lift and roll multi-ton driftwood logs that can crush a person. If the sand around a log is wet, the ocean can reach it.
  • Stay off jetties and wet rocks. Waves wash completely over jetties during storms, and algae-covered rock is extremely slippery.
  • Respect fences and closures. Cliff edges can be undercut and unstable after heavy rain. Don't climb barriers for a better photo.
  • Bring children and pets back from the edge, and keep an escape route uphill in mind.
  • Drive carefully. Expect high winds, standing water and fallen trees, and don't stop in traffic lanes to look at the waves.

Storm Watching Hotels on the Oregon Coast

Sometimes the best way to watch a storm is from behind a big window with a fireplace and a hot drink. Many oceanfront hotels lean into "storm season" with winter packages and lower midweek rates. A few well-known options, from north to south:

  • Lincoln City: The Coho Oceanfront Lodge and other cliff-top hotels look straight out at the surf.
  • Depoe Bay and Otter Rock: The Channel House and the Inn at Otter Crest put you close to spouting horns and the Devils Punchbowl.
  • Yachats: The Overleaf Lodge & Spa sits right on the rocky shoreline, with oceanfront rooms just yards from the waves and Cape Perpetua minutes away.
  • Florence: The Heceta Lighthouse Bed & Breakfast, in the restored keeper's house, has a view of one of the coast's most photographed headlands.
  • Bandon: Bluff-top hotels overlooking Face Rock are close to Shore Acres for day trips.

Book a room labeled "oceanfront," not just "ocean view," and ask for a higher floor for the best angle on the surf. Planning a longer trip? Our Oregon Coast road trip guide covers the full Highway 101 route.

What to Pack for Oregon Coast Storm Watching

Leave the umbrella at home; coastal wind will flip it inside out within minutes. Instead, bring:

  • A waterproof rain shell with a hood that cinches tight, plus rain pants.
  • Warm layers of wool or synthetic fleece (avoid cotton, which stays wet and cold).
  • Waterproof boots with good traction for wet viewpoints and trails.
  • Binoculars for watching the spray, seabirds and even migrating gray whales, which pass the coast from December through January.
  • For photographers: a rain cover, plenty of lens cloths and a fast shutter speed to freeze the spray. Salt spray is corrosive, so wipe down your gear afterward.

Oregon Coast Storm Watching FAQ

When is the best time for storm watching on the Oregon coast?

Storm watching season runs from November through March, and December and January usually bring the biggest and most frequent storms. The best individual days combine a large swell (15+ feet) with a high tide, especially during the winter King Tides.

Is storm watching in Oregon safe?

Yes, if you watch from elevated, fenced or enclosed viewpoints such as the Shore Acres observation building, the Depoe Bay seawall or the Seaside Promenade. Beaches, jetties and wet rocks are dangerous during high surf because of sneaker waves and rolling logs.

Where can I see the biggest waves in Oregon?

Shore Acres State Park near Coos Bay is famous for spray that shoots more than 100 feet into the air when winter swells hit its sandstone cliffs. Depoe Bay, Boiler Bay and Cape Perpetua near Yachats are also top spots.

What is the best town for storm watching in Oregon?

Depoe Bay is the easiest because you can watch the spouting horns from the downtown seawall. Yachats is a favorite for oceanfront lodging near Cape Perpetua, and Coos Bay or Bandon are the best bases for Shore Acres.

What are King Tides in Oregon?

King Tides are the highest tides of the year. They occur when a new or full moon coincides with the moon's closest approach to Earth, and in Oregon they arrive for a few days each November, December and January. The Oregon King Tides Project publishes the dates each year.

Does the Shore Acres observation building have a fee?

The observation building itself is free, but Shore Acres State Park requires a day-use parking permit. Check the Oregon State Parks website for current fees and hours.

How do I know if storm-watching conditions are good?

Check the National Weather Service surf forecast for swell height and period, and look for High Surf Advisories or Warnings. Compare those with NOAA tide tables, since big swells at high tide produce the most dramatic displays.

Can you see whales while storm watching?

Often, yes. Gray whales migrate south past the Oregon coast in December and January, so binoculars from viewpoints like Depoe Bay's Whale Watching Center can reward you with spouts between the storm waves.

Winter on the coast offers a spectacle no summer day can match: thundering surf, empty beaches, cozy oceanfront rooms and some of the lowest lodging rates of the year. Pick a safe viewpoint, check the surf forecast and enjoy the raw power of the Pacific.
