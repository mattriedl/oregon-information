Oregon grows more Christmas trees than any other state, so you never have to settle for a dry lot tree here. The best Oregon Christmas tree farms let you walk the rows, cut your own noble or Douglas fir and have it shaken and baled for the drive home, usually for $10 to $16 a foot. If you'd rather hunt in the woods, a $5 permit from a national forest lets you cut one yourself. Here are 2026 hours and prices, permit rules and tips.

Quick answer: Oregon Christmas trees at a glance

  • When: the farms in this guide open between Nov. 14 and Nov. 27, 2026, and most close around Dec. 20 to 22.
  • Cost at a farm: about $5 to $11 a foot for Douglas fir and $13 to $16 a foot for noble and Nordmann fir, so a 7-foot noble runs roughly $98 to $112.
  • Cost in a national forest: $5 per tree permit (plus a $2.50 fee online at Recreation.gov); fourth graders get one free through Every Kid Outdoors.
  • Best tree for heavy ornaments: noble fir. Best scent and lowest price: Douglas fir.
  • Keep it fresh: make a fresh cut and get it into water within 6 to 8 hours; give it about 1 quart of water per inch of trunk diameter.

Where Can You Get a Christmas Tree in Oregon?

You have three good options in Oregon: a U-cut (choose-and-cut) farm, a pre-cut tree from a farm or lot, or a wild tree cut on national forest land with a $5 permit. Each suits a different kind of outing.

  • U-cut farms are the classic family trip: you cut a shaped, living-room-sized tree with a borrowed saw, and the farm shakes and bales it.
  • Pre-cut trees at farms and lots are the fastest choice.
  • National forest trees are the cheapest and most adventurous, but wild trees are sparser and you'll drive forest roads in winter.

Oregon is the right place for any of them. According to Oregon State University Extension, Oregon sold 3.17 million Christmas trees in 2023, the most recent year with federal data, ranking first in the nation and adding $118 million to the state's economy. More than half of its trees are sold in California. Many farms sit in the Willamette Valley and the foothills of Clackamas County.

Noble Fir vs. Douglas Fir: Which Tree Should You Buy?

Choose a noble fir if you want strong branches for heavy ornaments and a tree that holds its needles; choose a Douglas fir if you want a soft, fragrant tree at the lowest price. Here's how Oregon's main species compare.

  • Noble fir: Oregon's best seller, with long "keepability" and stiff branches for heavy ornaments. It costs the most.
  • Douglas fir: soft, dark green to blue-green needles about 1 to 1½ inches long, with a sweet scent when crushed (National Christmas Tree Association). Bendier branches suit lighter ornaments, and it's the cheapest farm tree.
  • Nordmann and Turkish fir: newer species that make up a small but growing share of Oregon's crop. OSU Extension says they are known for holding their needles longer after harvest and, with steady water, can keep them four to six weeks indoors.
  • Grand fir: a soft-needled fir, usually priced at or below noble.

Measure your ceiling before you go, leaving room for the stand and topper; trees look smaller in a field.

How Do You Get an Oregon Christmas Tree Permit?

An Oregon Christmas tree permit costs $5 per tree from a national forest, and you can buy it online at Recreation.gov (plus a $2.50 service fee), at Forest Service offices or at local vendors such as many Bi-Mart stores. Permits for the 2026 season went on sale in mid-November. Nearly every national forest in Oregon sells them, from the Mt. Hood and Willamette to the Deschutes, Umpqua and Wallowa-Whitman.

  1. Buy one permit per tree. Mt. Hood, Willamette and Deschutes allow up to five per household; the Umpqua limited households to one last season.
  2. Print it. Online permits must be printed to be valid. On the Mt. Hood National Forest, the permit goes on your dashboard in clear view while you harvest and drive home.
  3. Tag the tree before you load it into your vehicle.

Fourth graders get a free permit. A student with an Every Kid Outdoors pass or voucher (everykidoutdoors.gov) can get one free tree permit; online, the $2.50 Recreation.gov fee still applies. On the Mt. Hood, fourth-grade permits are available at the Sandy and Zigzag district offices or online, not from vendors, and the student must be present at the office (USDA Forest Service, Mt. Hood).

A forest permit is good only on that forest's land, not private, state, tribal or county land; the Bureau of Land Management sells its own tree permits for participating field offices.

Is It Safe to Cut a Tree in the Mountains in Winter?

Yes, if you plan for winter conditions: forest roads are not plowed, cell service is spotty and daylight ends before 5 p.m. in December. The Forest Service recommends these precautions:

  • Carry tire chains, a shovel and a tow chain, start with a full tank and avoid deep snow. Check road conditions on ODOT TripCheck before you leave.
  • Bring a paper map, tell someone your plans, and leave before dark.
  • Parking in ODOT-plowed Sno-Parks, such as those around Mt. Hood, requires an Oregon Sno-Park permit.
  • Bring a hand saw, gloves, a measuring tape, rope and a tarp. Carry the tree out rather than dragging it.
  • Use caution in old wildfire burns, where dead trees can fall.

How to Keep Your Christmas Tree Fresh

The secret to a fresh tree is water: make a straight fresh cut, get the tree into water within 6 to 8 hours and never let the water drop below the base of the trunk. These steps come from the National Christmas Tree Association's care guide.

  1. Make a fresh cut. Remove about a ½-inch disk from the base, straight across (not angled or V-shaped).
  2. Get it into water fast. Most species can go 6 to 8 hours after cutting and still take up water.
  3. Use a big enough stand. It should hold about 1 quart of water per inch of trunk diameter. Don't whittle the trunk to fit.
  4. Check the water every day, especially in the first few days, when trees drink the most. Skip additives; OSU Extension says plain water is what matters.
  5. Keep it cool. Place the tree away from fireplaces, heaters, heat vents and direct sun, and use low-heat LED lights.

Fire safety: inspect light strings before use, don't overload circuits, turn lights off when you leave or go to bed, and never burn a Christmas tree in a fireplace or wood stove. Remove the tree once it is dry.

What to Do With Your Tree After Christmas

In Portland, a real tree goes out with your curbside compost: cut it into pieces 3 feet or shorter to fit in the bin for free, or set a whole tree up to 6 feet tall next to the bin for an extra charge ($5.45 last season). First remove all lights, ornaments, tinsel and the stand. Artificial and flocked trees go in the garbage. Apartment residents should check with their property manager (City of Portland). Elsewhere, check with your hauler.

Before you take the tree down, catch one more holiday tradition: Portland's 42nd annual tree lighting at Pioneer Courthouse Square is Friday, Nov. 27, 2026, 5:30 to 6:45 p.m. (Pioneer Courthouse Square). Our Portland Christmas lights guide covers the rest of the season's displays.

FAQ

How much does a Christmas tree cost in Oregon?

At U-cut farms near Portland, expect about $10 to $11 a foot for Douglas fir and $14 to $16 a foot for noble or Nordmann fir in 2026, so a 7-foot noble fir costs roughly $98 to $112. Some farms near Eugene charge less. A national forest permit is $5.

When do Christmas tree farms open in Oregon?

Most U-cut farms open in mid- to late November and close around Dec. 20 to 22. In 2026, Lee Farms opens Nov. 14, Pollard Ranch Nov. 21, and David Hill and Skyline Nov. 27.

Do I need a permit to cut a Christmas tree in Oregon?

You need a permit to cut a tree on national forest or BLM land. Permits cost $5 per tree. You don't need one at a Christmas tree farm.

What is the best Christmas tree to buy in Oregon?

Noble fir is Oregon's most popular tree because its stiff branches hold heavy ornaments and it keeps its needles well. Douglas fir is softer, more fragrant and cheaper.

How tall a tree can I cut in the Mt. Hood National Forest?

On the Mt. Hood National Forest, trees can be up to 15 feet tall, the stump must be 6 inches or less, and you can't cut within 100 feet of highways or trails or in wilderness areas.
